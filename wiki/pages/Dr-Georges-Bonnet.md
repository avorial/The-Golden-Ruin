# Dr. Georges Bonnet

<div class="wiki-layout">
<div class="wiki-main">

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>At The Table</h2>
<p>Dr. Georges Bonnet is one of those men who seems to have arranged his life exactly as he wishes it. He is a successful physician with a discreet private practice, an accomplished sculptor whose work appears in respectable exhibitions, and a familiar figure in the better artistic circles of the 17th arrondissement.</p>
<p>He is wealthy without ostentation, cultivated without seeming affected, and possesses the rare ability to make young artists feel that their work is being taken seriously. He is generous with criticism, introductions, and occasionally money. For all his charm, however, Bonnet remains strangely difficult to know. Questions about his family, childhood, or education receive an amusing story and a quiet redirection.</p>
<p>Bonnet is intelligent, patient, observant, and accustomed to being obeyed. He rarely raises his voice. When angry, he becomes quieter.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Public Face</h2>
<p>Bonnet appears to be a man in his late thirties or early forties. He is handsome in a severe way, with dark hair, pale skin, careful dress, and exceptionally steady hands. His clothing is conservative and expensive. He does not cultivate the theatrical appearance of the bohemian artist, dressing like a physician or prosperous professional even while working in his studio.</p>
<p>He speaks excellent French and German and enough English to converse comfortably with the American residents of the [[House of the North Star]]. His private practice serves wealthy patients suffering from exhaustion, nervous complaints, chronic pain, and conditions that benefit from discretion.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>The Sculptor</h2>
<p>Bonnet's sculpture is technically extraordinary. His portraits possess an uncomfortable degree of physical accuracy, capturing expressions his subjects did not realize they were making. His technique is strongly traditional but difficult to place within a single artistic generation. Some critics call him old-fashioned; others insist he can adopt nearly any school when he wishes.</p>
<p>He works primarily from life, employing professional models, dancers, actors, and students from private academies. One model was <strong>Eleanor “Nell” Whitcomb</strong>, an American dancer and former North Star resident.</p>
<blockquote>“A photograph records a surface. Sculpture records a presence.”</blockquote>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>The Physician</h2>
<p>Bonnet maintains his cabinet médical in the front rooms of [[Hôtel Bonnet]] on Rue Fortuny. He is attentive, discreet, and skeptical of fashionable treatments that produce no obvious result. He prescribes diet, rest, fresh air, controlled exercise, and attention to psychological disposition.</p>
<p>His particular medical obsession is aging, which he describes as the study of degeneration, vitality, and the gradual failure of the human body.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>North Star Connections</h2>
<p>Bonnet has known the North Star's directors for years and attends its dinners, exhibitions, and student presentations. He serves as an informal critic and guest instructor, treats American and British students seriously, and occasionally hires residents as models or assistants.</p>
<p>Nell Whitcomb remained in Paris after graduating and became one of his occasional models. Her recent death places Bonnet's connection to the North Star under uncomfortable scrutiny. The affair has climbed high enough within the police that [[Louis Lépine]] takes a personal interest.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Janine Du Bois</h2>
<p>Bonnet has recently become romantically involved with <strong>Janine Du Bois</strong>, a successful opera singer moving through Parisian theatrical and salon society. Janine believes him intensely private, somewhat eccentric, and more fragile than he wishes anyone to know. She suspects illness or an unhappy past, not the truth.</p>
<p>For perhaps the first time in many years, Bonnet is interested in constructing a future rather than merely continuing his present existence. He has begun sculpting Janine. The work is unfinished, and he refuses to exhibit it.</p>
</section>

## Personality

Bonnet dislikes fashionable cynicism and has little patience for people who mistake eccentricity for talent. His kindness toward gifted young artists can be genuine, but his generosity always carries a sense of evaluation. He is constantly deciding whether someone is interesting enough to deserve his time.

He is fascinated by ambition and distrusts anyone who claims not to fear death. Bonnet believes most people waste enormous portions of their lives and then complain that life is too short. He does not.

## Georges Bonnet Is 104 Years Old

Bonnet was born in **1786**. He has physically remained near forty for most of the nineteenth century, living through governments, revolutions, wars, fashions, artistic movements, and generations of friends.

The name Georges Bonnet is genuine, but the biography attached to it is not. He has presented himself as his own nephew, son, cousin, or successor. Portraits, exhibition catalogues, medical records, and private correspondence scattered across Europe show the same face decades apart.

## The Dreams

As a young physician, Bonnet suffered a prolonged fever and repeatedly dreamed of an unfamiliar city beside a dark lake. He saw unknown constellations, two strange suns, black towers, galleries of statues, and figures whose faces were hidden behind masks.

Someone in those dreams taught him a medical procedure. Bonnet first dismissed it as delirium, then tested what he remembered. The instructions worked. The word **Carcosa** appears in his earliest notebooks, though he does not remember learning it.

He believes the treatment arose from subconscious insight and his own medical genius. It did not. Something taught him.

## The Yellow Drug

Bonnet's longevity depends on a periodically administered compound developed from the dream procedure. It contains recognizable nineteenth-century chemicals, biological extracts, and ingredients with no apparent medical value. Some are imperfect terrestrial substitutes for substances he saw in Carcosa.

The treatment arrests the visible effects of aging. It does not cure aging. It suppresses something.

When a dose is late, the skin around Bonnet's eyes, fingernails, mouth, and veins turns yellow. The discoloration spreads until his body takes on an intense unnatural hue, followed by shaking, weakness, confusion, and apparent rapid aging. Bonnet believes his accumulated years are reasserting themselves. He is only partly correct.

The yellow episodes now come more frequently. He requires larger doses, and the interval between treatments is shrinking.

## Dr. Ludwig Weiss

Dr. Ludwig Weiss is one of the only living people who knows Bonnet's age. He helped refine and administer the treatment, initially believing Bonnet had made an extraordinary medical discovery. The formula, dreams, and physical changes have gradually horrified him.

Their friendship has become dependence. Bonnet needs Weiss because no one else understands the procedure. Weiss remains from guilt, loyalty, fear, and scientific fascination. He increasingly believes Bonnet did not discover the formula; something gave it to him.

## Eleanor “Nell” Whitcomb

Nell was an American dancer from the North Star's previous graduating class. She remained in Paris as a performer and model and sat for Bonnet several times.

During one sitting she saw him in the early stages of withdrawal: shaking hands, sickly eyes, and yellow skin around his mouth and fingers. Bonnet called it a liver complaint. Nell found contradictions in his biography, old catalogues containing his unmistakable style under other names, and an old likeness in which he appeared exactly as he does now.

She kept notes and may have taken a vial from his studio.

Nell confronted Bonnet. He tried charm, intimidation, and finally the argument that she had discovered one of history's greatest medical achievements. When she insisted on telling someone, he killed her.

The murder was not planned, a distinction that matters enormously to him. He tells himself Nell forced the situation and that one terrible act cannot erase a century of achievement.

## Louis Lépine's Inquiry

The death of a young American woman connected to an affluent international artists' residence threatens scandal, diplomatic embarrassment, and ugly press. Lépine treats it as a conventional murder complicated by rich foreigners and artistic secrecy.

Bonnet cooperates because innocence is easiest to perform in public. More importantly, he needs to learn what Nell told others before she died.

## Janine Du Bois

Janine knows nothing of Bonnet's age. She has seen him briefly ill but never fully yellow. Her love is genuine, as are his feelings for her.

After a century of watching people age and die, Bonnet had largely abandoned serious attachments. Janine changes that. He wants thirty years with her, then thirty more. She is therefore the strongest reason he cannot accept that the treatment is failing.

## What Bonnet Wants

Bonnet wants time. He wants Janine, unfinished work, future medicine, and the sculpture of another century. He believes mortality is a biological problem weaker men romanticize because they cannot solve it.

Nell's murder troubles him. It does not trouble him enough to surrender what he achieved.

## The Carcosan Truth

The drug was never intended to make Bonnet immortal. Longevity is a side effect. For decades it has been making his body suitable for something else.

His dreams grow clearer. The city feels closer. Paris streets briefly continue somewhere impossible. His sculptures contain faces he has never consciously seen. One unfinished figure bears the likeness of a woman associated with Carcosa, though Bonnet does not yet know her name.

## Playing Bonnet

Do not make Bonnet obviously sinister. He should initially be one of the most impressive and appealing people the characters meet in Paris: cultured, funny when he chooses, serious about art, respectful of talent, and genuinely generous. He can give a player character the best artistic advice they have ever received.

When the truth emerges, something valuable should feel corrupted. Even after exposure, Bonnet sounds reasonable:

> Human beings die because no one before me managed to prevent it. I did. Why should I surrender that achievement? Why should one death outweigh more than a century of life?

His tragedy is not that he cannot recognize evil. It is that after 104 years, he has become exceptionally skilled at explaining why every necessary thing he does is justified.
