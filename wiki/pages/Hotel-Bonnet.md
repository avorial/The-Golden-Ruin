# Hôtel Bonnet

## Address

**33 bis, rue Fortuny, [[17th Arrondissement]]**

The address is fictional, deliberately placed among the elaborate hôtels particuliers of Rue Fortuny. The street's carved façades, private courtyards, artists' houses, and wealthy residents make Bonnet look established rather than concealed. Nothing about his neighborhood suggests a starving artist, an illicit surgeon, or a manufacturer of dangerous drugs. It suggests success.

## Public Face

Hôtel Bonnet is the home and professional address of **Dr. Georges Bonnet**, a prosperous physician, fashionable sculptor, salon guest, and familiar figure in the affluent artistic circles around Plaine-de-Monceaux and Avenue de Wagram.

The limestone house presents two compatible reputations. Patients know Bonnet as a discreet practitioner trusted with nervous disorders, exhaustion, sleeplessness, private dependencies, and ailments a respectable family would rather not discuss in a hospital. Artists and patrons know him as a serious sculptor whose anatomical training lends unusual conviction to his portrait busts and figures.

A polished brass plaque beside the carriage entrance reads:

> **Docteur Georges Bonnet**  
> **Médecine Générale et Maladies Nerveuses**  
> **Consultations sur rendez-vous**

Bonnet calls the practice his **cabinet médical**, never a clinic. He sees wealthy patients by appointment and arranges his household so that someone seeking medical help can enter, wait, consult, and leave without crossing the family's private rooms or encountering the less decorous traffic of the atelier.

## The House

The property is built around a paved courtyard entered through tall carriage doors. The street wing contains Bonnet's public and domestic life. A lower rear wing holds the sculpture atelier. Beneath both runs a service cellar that appears ordinary until someone learns where to press, lift, or listen.

The architecture separates each of Bonnet's identities while allowing him to pass between them unseen:

- **Upper floor:** Bonnet's elegant private residence, bedrooms, dressing rooms, library, and a small private salon.
- **Street level, front:** entrance hall, waiting room, secretary's office, examination room, and consulting room.
- **Street level, rear:** formal salon and dining room, used for patrons, colleagues, and respectable dinners.
- **Courtyard wing:** a high-ceilinged sculpture atelier lit by north-facing glass.
- **Cellar:** wine, coal, storage, chemicals, medical apparatus, and locked cabinets containing the yellow drug.
- **Subcellar:** the hidden production room where Bonnet refines the drug and conducts his procedures.

The arrangement lets a carriage deliver a patient to the front door while a model, laborer, or crate of plaster enters through the service gate. At night, more troubling arrivals use the same practical distinction.

## The Cabinet Médical

The waiting room is quiet, expensive, and deliberately reassuring. Upholstered chairs stand far enough apart to discourage conversation. Medical journals share a table with art reviews. A clock ticks loudly. The secretary keeps appointments in a coded ledger that distinguishes ordinary patients, nervous cases, private referrals, and subjects invited after hours.

Bonnet's consulting room contains a broad desk, anatomical drawings, a locked medicine cabinet, and several small bronzes demonstrating that medicine is not his only profession. His examination room is clinical without appearing institutional: white enamel, polished wood, clean linen, a couch, scales, a reflex hammer, and a portable electrical apparatus used in respectable neurological treatments.

Patients see nothing overtly sinister. The unsettling details are matters of repetition. The same bitter odor clings to the examination room and the atelier. Several portrait busts resemble patients listed in the ledger. A cabinet holds more restraints than a private physician should require. Bonnet asks precise questions about dreams, color, memory, and the sensation of being watched from behind.

## The Formal House

The reception rooms insulate Bonnet's reputation from his work. Here he gives dinners, receives patrons, displays finished sculpture, and reassures influential friends that science and art remain perfectly civilized in his hands.

The salon favors deep green fabric, dark wood, framed studies, and flattering lamplight. A visitor invited only to dinner might never learn that the property extends beyond the courtyard. Servants direct patients toward the cabinet, dinner guests toward the salon, and models toward the side passage with such confidence that the divisions feel natural.

Bonnet's library is more revealing. Medical treatises stand beside works on anatomy, toxicology, mesmerism, degeneration, religious ecstasy, artistic genius, and the effects of drugs upon perception. The dangerous books are not hidden together. They are dispersed among reputable volumes, each carrying the same small ownership mark: a circle crossed by three descending strokes.

## The Sculpture Atelier

The rear atelier is a vast ground-floor room with a high ceiling, tall north-facing glass, a loading door, overhead pulleys, drains, and a floor built to bear clay, stone, plaster, armatures, and human weight. The arrangement is entirely credible for a successful sculptor. Heavy work remains at ground level, large pieces can move through the courtyard, and steady northern light lets Bonnet study a body without the distortion of direct sun.

Finished commissions occupy the cleanest wall: judges, physicians, industrialists, society women, and children rendered with impressive restraint. Farther into the room, the work becomes less public. Anatomical fragments crowd the shelves. Hands clutch at absent objects. Faces display expressions too private to have been posed. Several unfinished figures appear to twist away from something behind them.

Bonnet employs students and professional models, including promising young artists introduced through the [[House of the North Star]]. Most remember long sittings, excellent pay, and a physician's unnervingly exact interest in fatigue. A few lose portions of the evening. One insists that a clay figure changed posture while she slept.

The atelier is the house's hinge. It is still explicable, but it makes everything respectable at the front feel rehearsed. It also conceals three routes below:

- A broad stair to the ordinary cellar, used for coal, plaster, wine, and supplies.
- A hoist platform capable of lowering crates, sculpture, or an unconscious body.
- A narrow locked door behind a rack of armatures, leading to the laboratory stair.

## The Cellar Laboratory

The first cellar is absent from polite conversation but present on the official plans. Its wine bins, coal rooms, storage cages, and plaster supplies explain why servants and deliveries descend regularly.

Bonnet has converted the eastern rooms into a laboratory. Shelves hold acids, alcohol, ether, salts, dyes, fixatives, medical compounds, glass retorts, balances, burners, filters, and stoppered jars. Some bear legitimate suppliers' labels. Others use numbers that correspond to entries in Bonnet's appointment ledger.

A locked iron cabinet contains prepared doses of the **yellow drug** in several forms: amber ampoules, folded powders, pale tablets, and a viscous tincture that leaves a saffron stain around the stopper. Bonnet varies the preparation according to the subject and the effect he wants to observe.

The laboratory also contains evidence of method rather than mere vice:

- Temperature and pulse charts tied to coded patient initials.
- Photographs of subjects before, during, and after administration.
- Wax impressions of hands clenched during seizures or visions.
- Plaster death masks belonging to people who are officially alive.
- Failed batches sealed in a lead-lined box.
- Notes comparing artistic inspiration, nervous collapse, and susceptibility to a place identified only as **C.**

The laboratory door can be reached from the ordinary cellar, but its lock is excellent and the servants have been taught that Bonnet stores explosive chemicals inside.

## The Hidden Subcellar

The subcellar appears on no plans initially available to investigators. It predates the present house and may be a surviving service vault, quarry chamber, or fragment of an earlier property incorporated during construction. Bonnet found it when improving the atelier drains and had the entrance sealed behind a pivoting storage rack.

The stair descends farther than the dimensions of the house seem to allow. Its walls change from finished masonry to older stone. Pipes carry water and gas downward. A speaking tube connects the chamber to the atelier, allowing an assistant above to hear instructions or screams rendered faint by distance.

The principal room contains a tilting medical table, restraints, drains, gaslight, electrical leads, mirrors on movable frames, a camera stand, and a sculptor's rotating platform. Bonnet can examine a subject medically, photographically, and artistically without moving them from the center of the apparatus.

This is where he produces the most potent drug and conducts the procedures that combine dosage, sensory deprivation, reflected light, pain, suggestion, and artistic observation. He believes ordinary medicine merely suppresses symptoms. His procedure forces the hidden image behind a personality to become visible.

Sometimes he sees only terror.

Sometimes the subject describes Carcosa.

Sometimes the clay waiting upstairs changes before Bonnet returns to touch it.

## What the Plans Show

Plans obtained from municipal offices show the street house, courtyard, atelier, service cellar, and drainage. They do not show the laboratory conversion, concealed stair, or subcellar.

Careful comparison reveals three discrepancies:

1. The atelier's rear wall is thicker than structurally necessary.
2. A cellar ventilation shaft terminates on paper but continues to draw air.
3. The measured depth of the courtyard wing exceeds the interior cellar by nearly two metres.

An older contractor's invoice mentions “consolidation of pre-existing vaults” and a charge removed from the final public account.

## Household and Staff

Bonnet keeps enough servants to maintain appearances but not enough to lose control of the house.

- **Madame Clémence Vautrin, housekeeper:** Governs the residence, separates its visitors, and considers discretion a moral virtue. She knows about the laboratory but believes it supports legitimate medical research.
- **Émile Roussel, secretary:** Maintains appointments, correspondence, payments, and the coded patient ledger. He has begun copying certain entries because people return from evening appointments unable to remember him.
- **Luc Favre, atelier assistant:** Strong, technically capable, and devoted to Bonnet's art. He prepares clay, moves armatures, and lowers sealed crates. He knows the subcellar exists and refuses to enter it alone.
- **Dr. Henri Masson, occasional anesthetist:** A respectable colleague whose gambling debts make discretion purchasable. He tells himself that Bonnet's private procedures treat wealthy addicts and hysterics.
- **Margot Leclerc, model:** A student recruited through artistic acquaintances near the North Star. She survived one dose and now draws the same corridor beneath every building she enters.

## Bonnet and the North Star

Hôtel Bonnet lies close enough to the [[House of the North Star]] for Bonnet to move between them on foot. That proximity makes him part of the campaign's existing social fabric rather than a visitor imported from another quarter.

Bonnet can dine at the North Star, lecture on anatomy, advise students suffering from exhaustion, attend private exhibitions, and recommend promising models for paid work. He knows its directors, shares patrons with the house, and encounters the same doctors, artists, wealthy parents, and discreet intermediaries at neighboring salons.

His relationship with the North Star should initially feel useful. He treats a student's injured hand without charge. He identifies a toxin. He lends anatomical casts. He offers the atelier for a large sculpture. Only later does the pattern emerge: the students who interest him most are those whose work already seems touched by impossible landscapes, doubled suns, masked figures, or yellow light.

## Clues

- Bonnet's calling card appears among the possessions of a missing artist.
- A North Star student remembers posing at Hôtel Bonnet but cannot account for three hours after the sitting.
- Yellow residue beneath a victim's fingernails matches staining inside the laboratory cabinet.
- The secretary's ledger records appointments after midnight under the names of sculptures rather than patients.
- A society portrait in the salon resembles a corpse recovered weeks later, including an injury the sitter had not yet suffered.
- Coal deliveries exceed the needs of the house even in summer; the extra fuel heats the subcellar apparatus.
- A servant hears machinery beneath the courtyard when Bonnet is supposedly away.
- Bonnet owns municipal plans with the hidden rooms carefully cut away.

## Approaching the House

Investigators can enter Hôtel Bonnet through several social identities:

- **Patients:** Wealth, a referral, or convincing nervous symptoms secure an appointment in the cabinet médical.
- **Artists or models:** A portfolio, patron, or North Star introduction opens the atelier.
- **Dinner guests:** Society connections reveal the formal house but make private exploration difficult.
- **Tradespeople:** Chemical, coal, plaster, and medical deliveries use the courtyard and service routes.
- **Burglars:** The courtyard wall is manageable, but doors between the atelier and house are locked at night and Bonnet keeps a dog below stairs.
- **Officials:** A warrant reaches the official cellar. Finding what is not on the plans requires persistence before Bonnet's friends intervene.

## Scenes at Hôtel Bonnet

### The Respectable Consultation

Bonnet receives an investigator as a patient and accurately identifies a private fear. He offers relief in the form of a diluted yellow preparation. Refusal interests him more than acceptance.

### The Unveiling

At a dinner or private exhibition, Bonnet unveils a commissioned sculpture whose face resembles one of the investigators. The work was begun before they met him.

### The Missing Model

A North Star student disappears after accepting atelier work. Her coat remains upstairs, her shoes stand beside the modeling platform, and wet clay records a hand pressed outward from within the sculpture.

### The Night Delivery

Investigators watching the courtyard see a covered medical carriage arrive after midnight. The passenger walks in willingly but must be carried out before dawn.

### The Procedure

Bonnet captures or persuades a subject to enter the subcellar. During the procedure, everyone present briefly sees the room as an atelier in Carcosa, with Bonnet's unfinished works standing complete around them.

## Bonnet's Response to Intrusion

Bonnet relies first on status. He is a physician, property owner, successful artist, and friend to people whose complaints receive attention. He treats accusation as evidence of exhaustion or hysteria and offers to summon a colleague.

If investigators reach the atelier, he reframes disturbing work as artistic experiment. If they discover the laboratory, he invokes medical confidentiality and dangerous compounds. Only discovery of the subcellar forces him to act directly.

He does not immediately try to kill intruders. He wants to understand why they perceived what others overlook. A frightened investigator may become his most valuable subject.

## Connections

- [[17th Arrondissement]] places the house among affluent artists, physicians, and patrons.
- [[House of the North Star]] supplies social introductions, students, models, and gifted subjects.
- [[Café Guerbois]] and [[Chez le Père Lathuile]] provide older artistic contacts and neutral places to follow Bonnet's acquaintances.
- Medical suppliers, hospital colleagues, undertakers, photographers, and municipal clerks can each reveal one part of the house's hidden economy.

## Sources and Inspiration

- The address and Hôtel Bonnet are fictional.
- Rue Fortuny's late-nineteenth-century hôtels particuliers and artistic associations inform the setting.
- The ground-floor, north-lit atelier and heavy-service arrangement draw on surviving Parisian sculptors' studios, including the late-nineteenth-century studio associated with Antoine Bourdelle.

## The Yellow Drug

The precise origin of Bonnet's drug should remain adjustable to the campaign. He may have synthesized it from a corrupted medical formula, received its essential ingredient from an occult contact, extracted it from a substance that should not exist, or merely discovered how to make an older preparation clinically repeatable.

Its effects are not identical hallucinations. The drug weakens whatever ordinarily prevents a mind from recognizing Carcosan correspondences. Subjects interpret that recognition through their own fears and talents. A painter sees composition, a musician hears intervals, a physician sees anatomy, and a frightened witness sees pursuit.

Bonnet's true breakthrough is not the drug itself. It is the combination of dose, apparatus, setting, and observation that lets him reproduce the opening with increasing reliability.

## The Sculpture

Bonnet's most private works are not records of his subjects. They are anticipations. Under the procedure, he sometimes sculpts details before they occur: scars, deaths, transformations, or expressions a sitter will wear only upon seeing the finished piece.

He believes this proves that art can diagnose the future.

It may instead mean that each sculpture helps cause what it depicts.
