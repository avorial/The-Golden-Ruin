# Janine Du Bois

<div class="wiki-layout">
<div class="wiki-main">

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>At The Table</h2>
<p>Janine Du Bois is thirty-two, elegant, poised, and accustomed to being watched. She is a successful soprano known more for intelligence, dramatic presence, and emotional control than sheer vocal power. She is not the greatest singer in France and does not pretend to be. She is, however, very good.</p>
<p>Offstage, Janine is warmer and less theatrical than strangers expect. She has a dry sense of humor, dislikes flattery, and recognizes when a wealthy patron is trying to purchase affection rather than offer friendship. Her public manner is polished. Her private manner is direct.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>From Lyon to Paris</h2>
<p>Janine was born in Lyon to a respectable middle-class family. Her father, Étienne Du Bois, owned a successful textile concern and expected his daughter to marry well. Her mother, Claire, had studied piano and recognized Janine's musical ability early.</p>
<p>The household was conventional rather than bohemian, making Janine's pursuit of the stage an act of determination rather than necessity. She began formal training as a teenager, developed a clear soprano voice, excellent pitch, and unusual control, then persuaded her family to let her study seriously in Paris.</p>
<p>Lessons, examinations, rehearsals, diction, movement, and constant comparison taught her that talent was common and discipline was not. She developed both.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Career</h2>
<p>Janine built her career through small roles, understudy work, concerts, salons, and supporting parts barely worth a program credit. Conductors and directors learned that she arrived prepared, learned quickly, and created no unnecessary drama. That reputation became as valuable as her voice.</p>
<p>Her finest roles demand restraint: women who appear composed while concealing desperation, jealousy, fear, or grief. Critics sometimes call her voice “cool.” Janine considers it a compliment. She dislikes performers who substitute volume for feeling.</p>
<p>Her work now moves among opera, lighter theatre, private concerts, and society engagements. She earns enough to remain independent of patrons, an achievement she protects fiercely.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Society Favorite</h2>
<p>Janine is recognizable without being mobbed. Theatre patrons know her name, newspapers mention her, artists request sittings, and hostesses invite her because a successful soprano supplies glamour without quite carrying the scandal of more notorious stages.</p>
<p>She knows musicians, painters, writers, dancers, critics, costumers, managers, and wealthy patrons. She also knows which of them should not be trusted. She rarely gossips, but knows almost everything.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Nell Whitcomb</h2>
<p>Janine met [[Eleanor “Nell” Whitcomb]] after Nell's year at the [[House of the North Star]]. She initially mistook the American for a wealthy amateur, then saw her take poor engagements, accept criticism, attend lessons she could barely afford, and work.</p>
<p>Their friendship settled somewhere between sisters and mentor and student. Janine found auditions, recommended accompanists, lent costumes, and warned Nell away from predatory employers. Nell sometimes assisted backstage and attended rehearsals. Janine also introduced her to Georges Bonnet.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Georges Bonnet</h2>
<p>Janine met [[Dr. Georges Bonnet]] at a private dinner and expected a wealthy physician who collected artists. Instead, he was restrained, intelligent, knowledgeable about music, and uninterested in flattering her. He later attended a performance and asked to sculpt her. She declined, reconsidered, and eventually sat for him.</p>
<p>Sittings became conversations, conversations became dinners, and dinners became a relationship. Bonnet listens, respects her career as work, understands ambition, and never assumes marriage must end performance. Janine loves him and interprets his profound melancholy as loneliness.</p>
</section>

## Appearance and Personality

Janine is striking rather than delicate, with dark auburn hair, expressive eyes, and a controlled manner transformed by performance. She dresses beautifully without extravagance, favoring deep reds, dark greens, black, cream, and jewel tones that serve theatrical light. Her speaking voice is lower than strangers expect from a soprano, and she rarely raises it.

She is practical, emotionally guarded, and skeptical of declarations of genius. Talent impresses her; work impresses her more. Her generosity toward younger performers comes from knowing how easily ambition can be exploited.

## What Troubles Her

Bonnet disappears unexpectedly, cancels engagements without explanation, hates photographs, and turns irritable when asked about childhood. He sometimes recognizes music or recalls performances he claims never to have known. Once Janine joked that a correction was so precise he must have attended the old performance. He did not laugh.

She has also seen a strange yellow cast around his eyes and mouth during an illness he called a liver complaint. She believed him. Mostly.

## Nell's Death

Nell's murder devastates Janine. She feels responsible for making the introduction to Bonnet and remembers that Nell had become frightened in her final weeks. At first she assumes debts, a dangerous man, or some witnessed crime. Questions from [[Louis Lépine]] force her to reconsider details she dismissed.

Janine may approach current North Star residents herself. They are people who might care about Nell rather than treating her as another corpse in a police file.

## The Opera World

Janine can take investigators into rehearsal rooms, backstage corridors, costume workshops, private boxes, artists' entrances, managers' offices, patrons' salons, and late-night restaurants full of performers. She knows who fears losing a role, who owes money, who is having an affair, and which gentlemen mistake payment for discretion.

## An Ongoing Contact

Janine should survive the Bonnet affair and become a recurring ally. She continues performing and working after learning the impossible. If anything, she becomes harder to frighten. Strange costume designs, unexplained music, a patron's insistence upon an unused box, or singing in an empty theatre can bring her back to the North Star with a mystery of her own.

:::gm

## Janine Is Innocent

At the beginning, Janine knows nothing of Bonnet's true age, the yellow drug, Carcosa, or Nell's murder. Her confusion and grief are genuine. Do not make her behave mysteriously merely because the GM knows Bonnet's secret.

## Nell's Last Warning

Days before her death, Nell asked, **“Have you ever noticed that Georges never talks about being young?”** Janine laughed; Nell did not. Nell asked how long Janine had known him and whether she had met any member of his family.

Irritated by what sounded like an insinuation, Janine pressed her to speak plainly. Nell said, **“If I tell you something impossible, you will think I have lost my mind.”** Janine answered, **“Then wait until you can tell me something possible.”** It was one of their final conversations, and Janine deeply regrets it.

## Bonnet's Love

Bonnet genuinely loves Janine. This makes him more dangerous, not less. After a century of avoiding attachments to people who age and die, he wants a future with her. His dwindling treatment is therefore both a medical crisis and a threat to the life he imagines they might share.

He has considered telling her the truth. He has also considered giving her the treatment. He may eventually convince himself that administering it without her full understanding is an act of love.

## Breaking Point

Immortality horrifies Janine, but Nell's murder destroys her love. If Bonnet argues that Nell threatened everything he and Janine might have had, Janine understands immediately that he is trying to make her part of his justification. She refuses.

Once convinced, Janine turns against Bonnet decisively. She can exploit his affection, arrange a meeting, provide access to the house, or keep him talking while investigators act. She does not forgive him for loving her.

## After Bonnet

Janine loses Nell, discovers that the man she loved lived more than a century, and learns impossible things exist. She does not withdraw. She returns to the stage with fewer illusions and a clear idea of whom to contact when something unnatural begins moving through the opera world: the residents of the House of the North Star.
