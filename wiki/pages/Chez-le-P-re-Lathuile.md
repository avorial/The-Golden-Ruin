# Chez le Père Lathuile

![Chez le Père Lathuile at the edge of Place de Clichy.](/wiki/media/CLPL-Setting.png)

## Overview

[[Chez le Père Lathuile]] is an old restaurant and garden café at [[7 Avenue de Clichy]], close to [[Place de Clichy]] in the [[17th Arrondissement]]. Its reputation is older than many of the people who dine there. Families know it as a respectable address, artists know it as part of the mythology of Batignolles, and anyone conducting an affair knows there are tables where a conversation can disappear under leaves, lamplight, and the murmur of other dinners.

By 1890 it is no longer a fashionable novelty, and it is certainly not a disreputable cabaret. Père Lathuile has reached the more useful condition of being an institution. The garden is still its chief glory: trees and vines over white cloth, shaded tables spaced just far enough apart, waiters moving between them with the practiced blindness of men who have seen too much Paris to be startled by any of it. The food is good, the wine is better, and the staff remember exactly what should be remembered.

## Floor Plan

![Ground floor plan of Chez le Père Lathuile, Avenue de Clichy, circa 1890.](/wiki/media/CLPL-Floorplan.png)

The ground floor has two rhythms. Guests enter through the vestibule into the café room, the main dining room, or the garden terrace, where the restaurant presents itself as generous and open. The private cabinets sit close enough to the public rooms to seem innocent but far enough away to hold a confidential conversation. A patron can arrive visibly, dine respectably, and then withdraw without quite appearing to leave the evening.

The staff use a different map. The service entrance, kitchen, scullery, pantry, cellar stair, cashier's office, and service passage let waiters and kitchen hands cross the building along routes customers barely notice. That makes Père Lathuile ideal for meetings, surveillance, and mistakes: a letter left in a private room, a voice overheard from the service passage, a waiter who knows a guest was never where he claimed to be.

## History

The [[Lathuile Family]] were here before this part of the city felt like Paris. In the old Batignolles district they kept land and livestock, supplying milk, eggs, poultry, and other provisions to the growing city. As Paris crept outward, the family found that their position just beyond the old tax barrier had another advantage: wine could be sold more cheaply outside the city than within it.

So the farm became a **guinguette**, a country drinking house where Parisians came for afternoons of food, wine, dancing, and air that could still pretend to be fresh. By the end of the eighteenth century, Père Lathuile was already familiar to Sunday parties, soldiers, wedding guests, laborers, and prosperous families willing to cross the barrier for a cheaper bottle and a few hours of loosened manners.

## The Battle of Clichy

The restaurant's favorite legend dates to **30 March 1814**, when Coalition armies closed upon Paris and [[Marshal Bon-Adrien Jeannot de Moncey]] directed the defense around the [[Barrière de Clichy]]. Père Lathuile stood close enough to the fighting that later generations liked to find scars of the battle in the walls, whether or not the masonry agreed.

The story told inside the restaurant is simple and improves with wine. The proprietor opened his stores to the French defenders, fed them, and brought out the bottles rather than leave a single one for the Russians. A print of the defense still hangs where customers can see it. Every waiter knows the tale, though no two of them tell it with exactly the same emphasis.

## From Guinguette to Restaurant

As Batignolles urbanized, Père Lathuile changed with it. The old country drinking house became a proper restaurant, though the garden and family name remained. Cheap wine and wedding parties gave way to white tablecloths, elaborate dinners, private conversations, and customers arriving by carriage.

Painters and writers adopted the place as naturally as everyone else did. [[Édouard Manet]], [[Edgar Degas]], [[Claude Monet]], [[Pierre-Auguste Renoir]], [[Émile Zola]], and others in the Batignolles circles passed through Père Lathuile and the nearby [[Café Guerbois]]. The distinction became a joke among artists: one argued at Guerbois and ate at Lathuile.

## Manet's Garden

In 1879, [[Édouard Manet]] painted **[[Chez le Père Lathuile, en plein air]]** in the restaurant's garden, fixing one corner of the place into the mythology of modern painting.

![Édouard Manet's Chez le Père Lathuile, en plein air, painted in the restaurant garden in 1879.](/wiki/media/CLPL-Manet-Garden.jpg)

The young man leaning toward the fashionable woman is [[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]], a member of the family who worked at the restaurant. Visitors still ask where Manet placed his table. The staff generally give them an answer. Whether it is the same answer each time depends on the waiter and the tip.

## Père Lathuile in 1890

By 1890, the restaurant's great Impressionist years are already becoming history. Manet has been dead for seven years, painters once considered dangerous are becoming respectable, and younger artists increasingly drift north toward Montmartre. Père Lathuile remains busy anyway. Its age has become part of its appeal.

The dining room and garden draw prosperous bourgeois families, painters, actors, journalists, politicians, foreign travelers, discreet lovers, and young artists who cannot quite afford what they have ordered. Some come because of Manet, some because their parents came, and some because the waiters remember their preferred wine while forgetting everything else.

## The Dead Soldiers' Table

Among the staff there is an old story about a table in the garden, though nobody agrees which table it is. On certain cold nights near the end of March, after the restaurant has closed, one table is found laid for dinner: six places, six glasses, and a bottle of old Burgundy already opened. No member of the staff admits setting it.

If the glasses are cleared before sunrise, something unpleasant follows. A waiter falls ill, a customer dies unexpectedly, a bottle rack collapses, or a horse throws its rider outside the restaurant. Older staff prefer to leave the setting untouched until morning and blame the whole thing on soldiers killed near the [[Barrière de Clichy]] in 1814.

There is one detail they repeat less often. Sometimes there are **seven glasses**.

## Food & Drink

Père Lathuile is especially associated with poultry and wine. Its famous [[Poulet Père Lathuile]] anchors the menu, but the kitchens can produce the full comfort of a prosperous Parisian restaurant: oysters, fish, roast meats, game, sauces, vegetables, fruit, cheeses, pastries, and a cellar heavy with Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne, and fortified wines.

A wealthy patron can dine extravagantly. An artist known to the staff can sometimes dine much more cheaply. These are not always different people.

## Reputation

Père Lathuile wears several reputations at once. To respectable Paris it is old and reliable. To artists it is part of the history of modern painting. To lovers it is a garden with discreet tables. To journalists it is a place where someone important is always dining with someone interesting. To the superstitious, it is one of the places where the dead of 1814 have never entirely surrendered their table.

## People

[[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]] is one of the principal faces of the restaurant, a member of the family and the man most customers ask about Manet's table. [[Baptiste Renaud]], the senior waiter, has served there for decades and knows which stories are harmless, useful, or best left undisturbed. [[Madeleine Fournier]] keeps the cash desk and reservation books with a precision that gives her quiet power, while [[Émile Caron]] guards the kitchen's old dishes as though civilization depends upon them.

The younger staff are easier to overlook and often more useful. [[Camille Arnaud]] usually serves the garden and sees what customers leave behind. [[Colette Mercier]], a flower seller near the entrance, does not officially work for the restaurant, but she has watched the door long enough to become part of it.

## NPCs

### [[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]]

Louis is thirty-three, a maître d'hôtel, and a member of the owning family. He grew up inside Père Lathuile and knows its famous customers, family stories, scandals, debts, and which portions of those stories were invented for customers. At twenty-two he posed for [[Édouard Manet]] in the garden; eleven years later, people still ask him to identify the table in the painting. Sometimes he points to the right one. Sometimes he enjoys himself.

Louis is charming without becoming familiar and observant without appearing curious. He may forget what someone ordered, especially if it is convenient, but he rarely forgets who sat across from them.


### [[Baptiste Renaud]]

Baptiste is sixty-one and has worked at Père Lathuile for thirty-four years. He has served painters before they were famous, politicians before they were ministers, and actresses before they acquired respectable husbands. Almost nothing surprises him.

He knows the story of the Dead Soldiers' Table better than anyone still employed at the restaurant, but refuses to discuss it. If pressed, he says:

> "The dead have paid for their wine. There is no reason to trouble them."

Baptiste is the staff member most likely to protect someone from the supernatural without explaining what he is doing.


### [[Madeleine Fournier]]

Madeleine, thirty-eight, keeps the cash desk and the reservation books. She knows who dined at Père Lathuile, and more importantly, who paid. Her ledgers contain a private notation system: a dot for troublesome, a cross for generous, two lines for never seat him near his wife, a small circle for someone else regularly settles the account.

There is one mark that appears only six times in the ledger: a tiny star. Madeleine insists she did not make them.


### [[Émile Caron]]

Caron is forty-six and treats alteration of old recipes as an assault upon civilization. He knows almost nothing about painting and has little patience for the restaurant's artistic reputation. He does, however, know something unusual about the cellar. Once or twice each year, a bottle appears among the Burgundy that is not recorded in the cellar book. It has no label, only a wax seal marked with a small star. Louis has instructed him never to open one.


### [[Camille Arnaud]]

Camille is twenty-four, handsome, quick, discreet, and more ambitious than he appears. Painters occasionally tip him with sketches, which he keeps. Customers also leave letters, theatre tickets, calling cards, hotel keys, and other small objects behind; Camille keeps some of those as well.

Recently he found a drawing beneath the Dead Soldiers' Table. It shows the garden, six soldiers drinking, and a seventh figure standing behind them with no face.


### [[Colette Mercier]]

Colette, twenty-nine, does not officially work for Père Lathuile. She has simply occupied the entrance long enough to become part of it, and she sees everyone entering and leaving.

She claims that once each year, shortly before the anniversary of the Battle of Clichy, a young soldier approaches her and asks to buy six white flowers. His uniform is wrong for the present army. He always pays with an old coin. Colette has kept three of them.


## The Supernatural Truth

The popular explanation for the Dead Soldiers' Table is wrong. The six places do belong to soldiers who died during the defense of Clichy. The **seventh does not**.

Something was present at Père Lathuile during the fighting in 1814. The soldiers saw it, and at least one tried to bargain with it. The surviving fragments suggest they believed Paris might be saved if an offering was made before dawn. Whatever agreement they made failed. Paris surrendered, the soldiers died, and the seventh guest remained.

The annual meal is not a haunting so much as an invitation that was never withdrawn. The six dead men return because they are still trying to prevent the seventh guest from being served.


## Possible Yellow King Connection

The seventh figure does not need to be the King in Yellow. It may be more useful as something adjacent: a servant of Carcosa stranded in Paris, a human occultist who tried to use the battle as a ritual, an empty place waiting for someone from the campaign, or a phenomenon rather than a person.

The star appearing in Madeleine's reservation book creates an obvious connection to [[The House of the North Star]]. At first, keep it unclear whether the symbols share an origin or whether one has copied the other.


## Using Père Lathuile

Use Père Lathuile first as a social information exchange. Louis can arrange a fashionable introduction, Baptiste remembers older visits, Madeleine knows money and meetings, Camille has access to abandoned objects, and Colette sees arrivals and departures that were meant to pass unnoticed.

Let the supernatural material stay peripheral until the characters become curious. A first visit may contain nothing stranger than Baptiste refusing to seat anyone at an apparently excellent garden table. A second might reveal six unused wineglasses after closing. Only later should anyone notice that there are sometimes seven.
