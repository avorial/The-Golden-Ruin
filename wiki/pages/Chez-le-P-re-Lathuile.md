# Chez le Père Lathuile

![Chez le Père Lathuile at the edge of Place de Clichy.](/wiki/media/CLPL-Setting.png)

**Type:** Restaurant, garden café, former guinguette  
**Location:** [[7 Avenue de Clichy]], near [[Place de Clichy]]  
**District:** [[17th Arrondissement]]  


## Overview

[[Chez le Père Lathuile]] is one of those Paris establishments whose reputation is older than many of its customers.

Set near [[Place de Clichy]] at the beginning of [[Avenue de Clichy]], the restaurant occupies a site that has served food and drink to Parisians for generations. Its greatest attraction is its garden: trees, vines, shaded tables, white linen, lamps among the greenery, and enough separation between tables that a conversation may remain private even when the restaurant is crowded.

By 1890, Père Lathuile is neither a fashionable new restaurant nor a disreputable cabaret.

It is something better.

It is an institution.

Respectable families know it. Artists know it. Journalists know it. Actors know it. Men conducting affairs know which tables to request. The waiters know which guests should never be seated where they can see one another.

The food is good, the wine is better, and the staff have been watching Paris misbehave for longer than most of their customers have been alive.

## Floor Plan

![Ground floor plan of Chez le Père Lathuile, Avenue de Clichy, circa 1890.](/wiki/media/CLPL-Floorplan.png)

The ground floor is arranged around a public rhythm and a private one. Guests see the garden, terrace, main dining room, café room, vestibule, and private dining cabinets. Staff move through the service entrance, kitchen, scullery, pantry, cellar stair, cashier's office, and service passage.

This makes the restaurant useful for discreet meetings. A patron can dine visibly in the café room, withdraw to a private cabinet, or step into the garden without seeming to leave the establishment. Meanwhile, waiters and kitchen staff can cross the building by routes customers barely notice.


## History

The history of Père Lathuile begins before this district was properly part of Paris.

The [[Lathuile Family]] originally kept land and livestock in the old Batignolles district. Their farm supplied milk, eggs, poultry, and other provisions to the growing population of Paris.

As the city expanded toward them, the family discovered a more profitable use for their position.

Beyond the old tax barrier, wine could be sold more cheaply than within Paris. The Lathuiles therefore established a **guinguette**, a country drinking house where Parisians escaped the city for afternoons of food, wine, dancing, and fresh air.

By the end of the eighteenth century, Père Lathuile had become a familiar destination for Sunday excursions, wedding parties, soldiers, laborers, and increasingly prosperous Parisians looking for a few hours beyond the city's restrictions.


## The Battle of Clichy

The great legend of the restaurant dates from **30 March 1814**.

As Coalition armies closed upon Paris, [[Marshal Bon-Adrien Jeannot de Moncey]] directed the defense around the [[Barrière de Clichy]]. Père Lathuile became associated with the French defenders.

The proprietor opened his stores to the soldiers.

Food was distributed.

Wine was brought out.

According to the story repeated by generations of Lathuiles, the old proprietor declared that he would rather see French soldiers drink every bottle than leave a single one for the Russians.

Fighting came close enough to the establishment that later generations claimed scars of the battle could still be found in its walls.

A print of the defense of the Barrière de Clichy hangs prominently inside the restaurant.

Every waiter knows the story.

None tells precisely the same version.


## From Guinguette to Restaurant

As Batignolles became increasingly urban during the nineteenth century, Père Lathuile changed with it.

The old country drinking house gradually became a proper restaurant.

The garden remained.

So did the family name.

Wedding parties and cheap wine increasingly gave way to white tablecloths, elaborate dinners, private conversations, and customers arriving by carriage.

The restaurant also developed a strong association with painters and writers.

[[Édouard Manet]], [[Edgar Degas]], [[Claude Monet]], [[Pierre-Auguste Renoir]], [[Émile Zola]], and others connected with the artistic circles of Batignolles became associated with Père Lathuile and the nearby [[Café Guerbois]].

The distinction between the establishments eventually became a joke among artists.

One argued at Guerbois.

One ate at Lathuile.


## Manet's Garden

In 1879, [[Édouard Manet]] painted **[[Chez le Père Lathuile, en plein air]]** in the restaurant's garden.

The painting shows a young man leaning eagerly toward a fashionable woman seated beneath the trees.

The young man was [[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]], a member of the family who worked at the restaurant.

The painting transformed an already famous establishment into part of the mythology of artistic Paris.

Visitors still ask where Manet placed his table.

The staff generally give them an answer.

Whether it is the same answer each time depends upon the waiter.


## Père Lathuile in 1890

By 1890, the restaurant's greatest Impressionist years are already becoming history.

Manet has been dead for seven years.

Artists once regarded as dangerous are becoming respectable.

New painters increasingly gather farther north in Montmartre.

Père Lathuile nevertheless remains busy.

Its age is now part of its attraction.

Its customers include prosperous bourgeois families, painters, actors, journalists, politicians, foreign travelers, discreet lovers, and young artists who cannot quite afford the meal they have ordered.

Some customers come because of Manet.

Some come because their parents came.

Some come because they know the waiters will remember their preferred wine while forgetting everything else.


## The Dead Soldiers' Table

There is an old story among the staff concerning a table in the garden.

Nobody agrees which table it is.

The story claims that, on certain cold nights near the end of March, one table will be found prepared for dinner after the restaurant has closed.

There will be six places.

Six glasses.

A bottle of red wine already opened.

No member of the staff admits setting it.

The wine is always an old Burgundy.

If the glasses are cleared before sunrise, something unpleasant is said to happen.

A waiter falls ill.

A customer dies.

A bottle rack collapses.

A horse throws its rider outside the restaurant.

For this reason, some of the older staff simply leave the setting untouched until morning.

The story is usually blamed upon the soldiers killed near the Barrière de Clichy in 1814.

There is another detail less frequently repeated.

Occasionally there are **seven glasses**.


## Food & Drink

Père Lathuile is particularly associated with poultry and wine.

Its most famous dish is [[Poulet Père Lathuile]], a chicken preparation closely associated with the restaurant.

The kitchens also serve the expected dishes of a prosperous Parisian restaurant: oysters, fish, roast meats, game, sauces, vegetables, fruit, cheeses, pastries, and a substantial cellar of Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne, and fortified wines.

A wealthy patron can dine extravagantly.

An artist known to the staff can sometimes dine considerably more cheaply.

These are not always different people.


## Reputation

Père Lathuile has several reputations simultaneously.

To respectable Paris, it is an old and reliable restaurant.

To artists, it is part of the history of modern painting.

To lovers, it is a place with a garden and discreet tables.

To journalists, it is a place where someone important is always dining with someone interesting.

To the superstitious, it is one of the places where the dead of 1814 have never entirely surrendered their table.


## People

- **[[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]]**: Member of the Lathuile family and one of the principal faces of the restaurant.
- **[[Baptiste Renaud]]**: Senior waiter who has served at Père Lathuile for decades.
- **[[Madeleine Fournier]]**: Cashier and keeper of the reservation books.
- **[[Émile Caron]]**: Chef responsible for preserving the restaurant's traditional dishes.
- **[[Camille Arnaud]]**: Young waiter who usually serves the garden.
- **[[Colette Mercier]]**: Flower seller frequently found near the restaurant entrance.

## NPCs

### [[Louis Gauthier-Lathuile]]

**Age:** 33  
**Role:** Maître d'hôtel and member of the owning family  
**Historical NPC**

Louis grew up inside Père Lathuile.

He knows its famous customers, family stories, scandals, debts, and precisely which portions of those stories are inventions.

At twenty-two he posed for [[Édouard Manet]] in the restaurant garden. Eleven years later, customers still ask Louis to identify the exact table shown in the painting.

Sometimes he points to the right one.

Sometimes he enjoys himself.

Louis is charming without familiarity and observant without appearing curious.

Most importantly, Louis remembers faces.

He may forget what someone ordered.

He rarely forgets who sat across from them.


### [[Baptiste Renaud]]

**Age:** 61  
**Role:** Senior waiter  
**Fictional NPC**

Baptiste has worked at Père Lathuile for thirty-four years.

He has served painters before they were famous, politicians before they were ministers, and actresses before they acquired respectable husbands.

Almost nothing surprises him.

He knows the story of the Dead Soldiers' Table better than anyone still employed at the restaurant.

Baptiste refuses to discuss it.

If asked directly, he says:

> "The dead have paid for their wine. There is no reason to trouble them."

Baptiste is the staff member most likely to protect someone from the supernatural without explaining what he is doing.


### [[Madeleine Fournier]]

**Age:** 38  
**Role:** Cashier and reservations clerk  
**Fictional NPC**

Madeleine knows who dined at Père Lathuile.

More importantly, she knows who paid.

Her reservation books contain a private notation system.

A dot means troublesome.

A cross means generous.

Two lines mean never seat him near his wife.

A small circle means someone else regularly settles the account.

There is another mark that appears only six times in the ledger:

a tiny star.

Madeleine insists she did not make them.


### [[Émile Caron]]

**Age:** 46  
**Role:** Chef  
**Fictional NPC**

Caron treats alteration of old recipes as an assault upon civilization.

He knows almost nothing about painting and has little patience for the artistic reputation of the restaurant.

He does know something unusual about the cellar.

Once or twice each year, a bottle appears among the Burgundy that is not recorded in the cellar book.

The bottle has no label.

Its wax seal bears a small star.

Caron has been instructed by Louis never to open one.


### [[Camille Arnaud]]

**Age:** 24  
**Role:** Garden waiter  
**Fictional NPC**

Camille is handsome, quick, discreet, and more ambitious than he appears.

Painters occasionally tip him with sketches.

He keeps them.

Customers also leave letters, theatre tickets, calling cards, hotel keys, and other objects behind.

Camille keeps some of those too.

Recently he found a drawing beneath the Dead Soldiers' Table.

It depicts the garden.

Six soldiers sit drinking around the table.

A seventh figure stands behind them.

The figure has no face.


### [[Colette Mercier]]

**Age:** 29  
**Role:** Flower seller  
**Fictional NPC**

Colette does not officially work for Père Lathuile.

She has simply occupied the entrance long enough to become part of it.

She sees everyone entering and leaving.

She also claims that once each year, shortly before the anniversary of the Battle of Clichy, a young soldier approaches her and asks to buy six white flowers.

His uniform is wrong for the present army.

He always pays with an old coin.

Colette has kept three of them.


## The Supernatural Truth

The popular explanation for the Dead Soldiers' Table is wrong.

The six places do represent soldiers who died during the defense of Clichy.

The **seventh does not**.

Something was present at Père Lathuile during the fighting in 1814.

The soldiers saw it.

At least one of them attempted to bargain with it.

The surviving fragments of the story suggest that the men believed they could prevent the fall of Paris if they offered it something before dawn.

Whatever agreement was made failed.

Paris surrendered.

The soldiers died.

The seventh guest remained.

The annual meal is not a haunting.

It is the continuation of an invitation.

The six dead men return because they are still attempting to prevent the seventh guest from being served.


## Possible Yellow King Connection

The seventh figure should not immediately be identified with the King in Yellow.

More useful possibilities are:

1. **A servant of Carcosa** who became stranded in Paris long before anyone recognized what it was.
2. **A human occultist** who saw Carcosa and attempted to use the battle as a ritual.
3. **An empty place** that eventually becomes occupied by someone from the campaign.
4. **A phenomenon rather than a person**, with the seventh glass representing someone who has not yet arrived.

The star appearing in Madeleine's reservation book creates an obvious connection to [[The House of the North Star]], but it should initially be unclear whether the symbols share an origin or whether one has copied the other.


## Using Père Lathuile

Père Lathuile should function primarily as a **social information exchange**.

A character needing a fashionable introduction speaks with Louis.

A character investigating someone who visited years ago speaks with Baptiste.

Someone following money or secret meetings needs Madeleine.

Someone looking for an object accidentally left behind needs Camille.

Someone asking who entered or departed without attracting attention needs Colette.

The supernatural material should remain peripheral until the characters become curious.

A first visit may contain nothing stranger than Baptiste refusing to seat anyone at an apparently excellent garden table.

A second visit might reveal six unused wineglasses after closing.

Only much later should anyone notice that there are sometimes seven.
