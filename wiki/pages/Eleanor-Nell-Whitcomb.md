# Eleanor “Nell” Whitcomb

<div class="wiki-layout">
<div class="wiki-main">

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>At The Table</h2>
<p>Eleanor Whitcomb was almost never called Eleanor. To her family she was <strong>Nellie</strong>; to her friends at the [[House of the North Star]], simply <strong>Nell</strong>.</p>
<p>She was twenty-four when she died. Nell was lively, stubborn, observant, and considerably more serious about becoming an artist than her cheerful manner suggested. She laughed easily, made friends quickly, and possessed the useful talent of getting people to tell her things they had not intended to say.</p>
<p>She was not considered a great beauty, but people remembered her: a dancer's posture, expressive dark eyes, chestnut hair, and one foot slightly turned outward even while waiting for coffee.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Coming to Paris</h2>
<p>Nell came from a prosperous old Boston family and arrived in Paris ostensibly to study drawing, art history, French, music, and movement. Her parents approved of the North Star because it offered instructors, meals, expectations, respectable associations, and other young Americans and Britons rather than leaving their daughter unattended in Montmartre.</p>
<p>The Whitcombs considered it an acceptable finishing experience for an artistic daughter. Nell regarded it as an escape.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Nell at the North Star</h2>
<p>Nell joined the previous year's class as an illustrator. She was competent rather than exceptional, but she understood bodies far better than landscapes or still life. Her sketches of dancers, horses, rain-soaked pedestrians, reaching waiters, and falling bodies captured movement with startling economy.</p>
<p>She took movement and ballet lessons outside the house, initially claiming they improved her figure drawing. That was true, but incomplete. Nell loved dancing.</p>
<p>She was affectionate, disruptive, and hardworking: the resident most likely to lead three friends across Paris after dinner for an obscure performance, return just before curfew, misplace a library book, argue with an instructor, befriend a servant, and then spend the next afternoon repeating one drawing until she understood it.</p>
<blockquote>“I shall become an artist,” another student would announce.<br /><br />“Then become one,” Nell would reply.</blockquote>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Becoming Professional</h2>
<p>Nell knew she had begun too late for the Paris Opéra school, but Paris offered theatres, operettas, spectacle companies, music halls, touring productions, and private entertainments. She trained relentlessly and found paid ensemble work by the end of her residency.</p>
<p>Her parents expected her home after graduation. Instead she announced she would remain another year. When her father threatened to stop sending money, she replied that this seemed fair enough and began supporting herself. Mostly.</p>
<p>She shared a modest apartment with another theatrical young woman, danced wherever she could, substituted for injured performers, and modeled because it paid better than minor theatrical engagements. She was not becoming famous. She was becoming professional. The distinction mattered to her.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Janine and Bonnet</h2>
<p>The theatrical world introduced Nell to <strong>Janine Du Bois</strong>, a successful opera singer who liked the American's determination and lack of pretension. Their relationship settled somewhere between friendship and mentorship, with Janine helping Nell find work and Nell assisting with costumes, errands, and rehearsals.</p>
<p>Janine brought her into the orbit of [[Dr. Georges Bonnet]]. Bonnet sought dancers as models for their control of posture and musculature. He paid well, remembered Nell's work from a North Star exhibition, and discussed sculpture rather than treating her as furniture. Nell initially considered the arrangement an extraordinary piece of luck.</p>
</section>

## The Whitcomb Family

The Whitcombs are respectable rather than enormously rich. Their money comes from shipping, insurance, and investments accumulated over generations.

- **Charles Winthrop Whitcomb**, her father, serves on charitable and commercial boards and considers himself a man of responsibilities rather than ambitions.
- **Margaret Lowell Whitcomb**, her mother, comes from another established Boston family and prizes education, music, and proper society.
- **Charles Whitcomb Jr.**, the eldest brother, followed his father into business.
- **Edward “Ned” Whitcomb**, three years older than Nell, attended Harvard and spent enough time in New York and Europe to be less horrified by his sister's choices. He secretly sent her money twice.

The family expected Eleanor to become educated, cultivated, and appropriately married. They did not expect a professional dancer.

## An American Connection

The Whitcomb name should be familiar to an American investigator from Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Harvard circles, or the established professional classes. Their parents may have attended the same dinners, served on the same boards, or donated to the same museums and lectures. A military character might know Ned through Harvard acquaintances, travel, or a militia organization.

When [[Louis Lépine]] says, **“Eleanor Whitcomb of Boston,”** someone should immediately understand that she was not an anonymous Parisian dancer. Their people may know her people. Her death becomes personal before the investigation properly begins.

## What the North Star Remembers

Nell has been gone from the house for almost a year, but traces remain. Madame Voclain remembers precisely how she took her coffee. A staff member still complains about a book she never returned. Her initials are scratched beneath a studio table. An older resident remembers dance steps demonstrated in the dining room. An unfinished figure drawing may remain among stored student work.

The investigation should not merely lead the characters to a corpse. It should let them discover **Nell**.

## Nell Starts Asking Questions

During one sitting at [[Hôtel Bonnet]], Bonnet dropped a sculpting tool. His hands trembled and yellow discoloration marked his fingers, eyes, and mouth. He called it a recurring liver complaint. Nell believed him for roughly two days.

She asked how long he had exhibited and received answers that contradicted his stories. Old catalogues revealed sculptures in his unmistakable style produced decades earlier. An old photograph or likeness showed a man identical to Bonnet at a date when he should not have been old enough to appear in it.

Nell stopped suspecting artistic plagiarism and began investigating something impossible.

## Her Last Weeks

Nell returned to the North Star several times without explaining why. She asked to use the library, borrowed material on nineteenth-century exhibitions and medical history, and spoke privately with at least one staff member.

She told one friend, **“There is something wrong with Bonnet.”** On another occasion: **“I don't think he is sick. I think he is trying very hard not to become sick.”**

Her notebook filled with dates, names, addresses, and sketches. One page contains only:

> **YELLOW AGAIN**

underlined twice.

She may also have stolen a small vial from Bonnet's medical rooms. If so, it is now missing.

## The Unfinished Letter

Nell's parents never accepted her decision to remain in Paris, but they did not disown her. Their letters grew strained; her mother continued sending occasional money despite her father's objections.

Among Nell's possessions is an unfinished letter to Margaret. Nell admits that dance will probably never make her famous and then writes that this does not matter. For the first time, she is doing something because she chose it.

## The Absent Investigator

Nell reached the mystery first. Her notes let the characters follow her path without handing them her conclusions. Useful fragments include:

- Dates of Bonnet exhibitions that cannot belong to one ordinary lifetime.
- A sketch of his yellowed hand made from memory.
- Names of elderly patrons who remember “his father” looking exactly like him.
- A rubbing of the ownership mark from one of Bonnet's early notebooks.
- A North Star library slip for medical works on degeneration and vitality.
- A theatre program with Janine's dressing-room address.
- A reference to the missing vial disguised as “the yellow costume button.”

## GM Use

Nell should feel like someone who could have been one of the current investigators. She arrived with money, expectations, artistic ambitions, and parents who trusted the North Star to keep her safe. A year later she was living independently, working professionally, and investigating the impossible.

Then Bonnet murdered her.

That is why her death should disturb the North Star so deeply.

## GM Ideas

- A current resident remembers hiding Nell's notebook but not where.
- Ned Whitcomb arrives from America determined to take her body and papers home.
- Janine recognizes an appointment in Nell's diary and realizes she made the introduction.
- The missing vial was given to someone at the North Star for safekeeping.
- Bonnet's unfinished sculpture of Nell changes pose as the investigation advances.
