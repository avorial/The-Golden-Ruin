# Dr. Ludwig Weiss

<div class="wiki-layout">
<div class="wiki-main">

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>At The Table</h2>
<p>Dr. Ludwig Weiss is an elderly Austrian physician whose age seems to have settled into him rather than defeated him. He is thin, stooped, white-haired, and immaculately dressed in clothes several years behind Parisian fashion. His hands tremble slightly at rest, then become unexpectedly steady over an instrument or a page of clinical notes.</p>
<p>Weiss gives the impression of a man who has spent most of his life in rooms lined with books. He is courteous, formal, and deeply uncomfortable with unnecessary intimacy. Unlike [[Dr. Georges Bonnet]], who enjoys admiration, Weiss seems embarrassed by attention.</p>
<p>He speaks with precision and dislikes exaggeration. When frightened, he becomes more precise still.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Public Reputation</h2>
<p>Weiss is respected in certain medical circles for work on degenerative disease, endocrine function, rejuvenation therapies, experimental chemistry, aging, and nervous disorders. He has published in German and French journals and once held a hospital appointment in Vienna.</p>
<p>His work is accomplished but unfashionable. Younger doctors sometimes describe him as belonging to an older generation of experimental medicine. He has never become famous and prefers it that way.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Manner</h2>
<p>Weiss is not warm, but neither is he cruel. He prefers facts to reassurance and is more likely to explain exactly what is happening to a dying patient than offer false comfort. He distrusts charm, making him one of the few people in Paris almost entirely unimpressed by Bonnet.</p>
<p>He wears spectacles for reading and removes them when irritated. While deciding how much truth to tell, he polishes them with slow and conspicuous care.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Georges Bonnet</h2>
<p>Weiss has known Bonnet for decades. Publicly, they are old professional colleagues: Weiss consults on difficult cases and helps acquire specialized chemicals and equipment. Their conversations sound less like those of colleagues than brothers who have spent too many years arguing over the same questions.</p>
<p>Weiss is one of the few people who tells Bonnet he is wrong. Bonnet is one of the few people Weiss permits to raise his voice at him. Their friendship is genuine. So is their resentment.</p>
<p>Weiss calls him simply, “Georges.” Almost no one else does.</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>In Paris</h2>
<p>When in Paris, Weiss stays in a modest but respectable hotel or furnished apartment near [[Hôtel Bonnet]]. He visits the house privately, knows its medical rooms, laboratory, cellar, and concealed passages, and is admitted by servants who understand that he must not be disturbed.</p>
<p>Weiss regards the [[House of the North Star]] with mild skepticism. He considers artists overly dramatic but useful observers: a painter notices color, a sculptor posture, a dancer imbalance, and a writer contradiction. He is especially interested in anyone who knew [[Eleanor “Nell” Whitcomb]].</p>
</section>

<section class="wiki-section">
<h2>Nell and the Police</h2>
<p>Weiss met Nell only a handful of times. Her direct questions impressed and unsettled him, particularly when she asked about Bonnet's health. He gave an evasive answer and later regretted it. By the time he understood how close she was to the truth, she was dead.</p>
<p>He dislikes police attention because decades of professional discretion conceal choices that could destroy his reputation and liberty. [[Louis Lépine]] makes him visibly nervous, and Lépine notices.</p>
</section>

## Appearance

Weiss is tall but bent with age, with deep-set pale eyes, a narrow face, and a close-trimmed white beard. He wears conservative dark clothing and carries a silver-headed cane for balance rather than display. His fingers smell faintly of tobacco, alcohol, antiseptic, or chemical reagents.

## Bonnet's True Age

Weiss knows that Georges Bonnet is **104 years old**. He has watched Bonnet remain physically unchanged while everyone around them aged, witnessed the yellowing, administered the treatment, and helped refine it. He knows exactly what happens when Bonnet goes too long without a dose. He does not know what Bonnet is becoming.

## The Beginning of His Ruin

Weiss first encountered Bonnet as a younger physician fascinated by reports of rejuvenation and arrested aging. Bonnet had already experimented for years. Weiss assumed fraud until he examined Bonnet, studied the records and photographs, and watched him deteriorate without the drug.

Weiss was not coerced. He helped because he wanted to understand the impossible. He told himself that refusing to investigate would be cowardice, that the treatment might benefit humanity, and that scientific knowledge was morally neutral. This willing beginning is the foundation of his guilt.

## Refining the Formula

Bonnet's preparation was crude and dangerous. Weiss standardized it, improved purification, adjusted dosage, removed poisonous impurities, made injections safer, and lengthened the interval between treatments. Without him, the experiment might have ended naturally decades ago.

Bonnet knows the broad formula but not all of Weiss's refinements. Weiss kept several steps in private notation, partly from habit, partly as a precaution, and partly to retain one measure of control. His failing health now makes that dependence dangerous to both men.

## Dreams, Chemistry, and Carcosa

Weiss knows the formula came from Bonnet's dreams. For years he treated this as fever-born symbolism masking unconscious reasoning. Yet parts of the formula should be useless, mutually obstructive, or founded on assumptions no European chemist would make. Together, they work.

He has seen **Carcosa** in Bonnet's oldest notes. Searches through literary, occult, and antiquarian sources produced contradictory descriptions of a city, a kingdom, or a place that does not exist. Weiss stopped searching because he no longer wants confirmation.

> “Georges believes he found the formula. I think the formula found him.”

## The Yellowing

The yellow coloration is not jaundice. It behaves more like pigmentation and appears within tissue. Years ago, Weiss took a small skin sample during an episode and found structural changes under magnification that he could not explain. He destroyed the sample and never told Bonnet.

Weiss now believes the drug produces two effects: it prevents ordinary aging while gradually altering Bonnet's physiology. The longer the treatment continues, the less human Bonnet becomes. He cannot prove it and is terrified that he is right.

## Nell Whitcomb

Before her death, Nell confronted Weiss about Bonnet's yellowing and impossible history. She asked one question: **“How old is he?”** Weiss called the idea absurd. Nell knew he was lying.

He could have warned her, sent her away, or directed her to the police. He did none of those things. Weiss suspected Bonnet almost immediately after Nell died. Their relationship nearly collapsed, but exposing Bonnet would also expose Weiss's research, complicity, and decades of silence. He hesitated again.

## His Current Crisis

Weiss suffers weakness, dizziness, numbness, and periods when his hands refuse to cooperate. Bonnet still relies upon him for crucial stages of the treatment. Weiss wants it ended but does not necessarily want Bonnet dead. He still loves Georges after decades of shared work, resentment, guilt, dependence, and fear.

Weiss knows Bonnet will not stop willingly. He also fears Bonnet may eventually value the knowledge in Weiss's head more than the life of his friend.

## Layers of Disclosure

Weiss never confesses everything at once. He reveals the truth in stages:

1. Bonnet has a rare chronic medical condition.
2. The condition is connected to an experimental treatment.
3. The treatment has existed for decades.
4. Bonnet has existed decades longer than he should have.
5. The formula was not invented in any conventional sense.

Trust, convincing evidence, or shared danger moves him from one layer to the next. Pressure alone makes him retreat into medical exactitude.

## Breaking Point

Nell's death damaged the friendship. A second young victim ends it. If Bonnet threatens another person to preserve himself, Weiss gives the investigators his notes, explains the treatment, and helps them stop Bonnet even if it means exposure, prison, or Bonnet's death.

## Playing Weiss

Weiss is not a convenient source of answers. He knows too much and wants to say too little. He is ashamed, afraid, and still proud of the work. He does not regret discovering the impossible. He regrets what they chose to do afterward.

His tragedy is that he spent decades believing superior understanding would tell him where to stop. It did not.
