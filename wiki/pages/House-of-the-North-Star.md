# House of the North Star

![The discreet street frontage of the House of the North Star.](/wiki/media/HotN-Street-View.png)

## Overview

The House of the North Star is a private artists' retreat set behind a discreet Parisian frontage on Rue Theodule Ribot, near Avenue de Wagram. From the street it looks like another respectable residence of carved stone, wrought iron, shut shutters, and correct silence. Inside, it is a deep courtyard house arranged for instruction, separation, and observation.

Among old families, the house is known as a place where young talent can be disciplined, refined, and protected from vulgar attention. Its owner inherited the property after his father's suicide and later transformed it from a mourning household into a sanctuary for painters, musicians, poets, and difficult children with inconvenient brilliance.

Among patrons, the retreat has a reputation for discretion. A family may send a son who hears music no tutor can explain, a daughter whose sketches unsettle guests, or a restless heir who needs culture, distance, and silence. The house promises instruction, quiet, and the company of others born beneath the same cold privileges.

## History

The estate came into its current life through bereavement. Its present master was still young when his father killed himself, leaving behind a house filled with shuttered rooms, unpaid correspondence, and the kind of silence servants learn not to disturb. His mother, undone by grief, found her only comfort in the paintings her son began making for her: small northern landscapes at first, then night skies, frozen harbors, bare trees, and windows lit against an impossible dark.

Those paintings became the language of the house. His mother sat with them for hours, claiming each canvas gave her a fixed point to look toward when memory began to turn. Guests who visited during that period remembered the boy as courteous, pale, and unnervingly patient, more host than child, always ready with tea, a blanket, or another study of the same distant star.

Years later, while traveling among wealthy families in America, he discovered how many great houses had children they did not understand. Some were artistic. Some were scandalous. Some had seen or said things no drawing room could comfortably contain. He offered a solution with the mild confidence of a man proposing charity: let the children come to his house for a season. Let them be surrounded by beauty, discipline, and one another. Let them learn to turn their strangeness into art.

The first invitations were personal favors, extended through dinners, gallery openings, and long train conversations with mothers who were tired of apologizing for their children. By the second year, families were asking for places before they were offered. The House of the North Star became less a household than an understanding: a refuge for the aristocracy's gifted, grieving, and troublesome heirs.

## The Name

The name began with his mother. After her husband's death, she said her son's paintings gave her a north star: not warmth, not happiness, but direction. The image pleased him, and he began signing certain works with a small star in the upper margin, bright enough to notice only after the rest of the painting had already taken hold.

When he opened the house to young artists, he kept the phrase. To patrons, it suggested guidance, steadiness, and noble purpose. To the students, it became a promise that one could be lost without being abandoned. The house would not rescue them from the dark. It would teach them how to navigate by it.

## House & Grounds

![Ground floor plan of the House of the North Star.](/wiki/media/HotN-Floorplan.png)

The house is larger than its narrow street presence suggests. Its public face opens into reception rooms and galleries, but the true center is the main courtyard, a bright interior void around which the household circulates. Voices carry strangely there. A piano note played in the salon may be heard by someone crossing from the kitchen, while a whisper from the landing can seem to arrive from above.

The western side of the house holds the formal rooms: dining room, main salon, small salon, and the rooms where patrons are entertained before they are permitted to meet the students. The long gallery and vestibule form the spine of the house, controlling movement between the public rooms, private bedrooms, service areas, and courtyards.

The southern rooms along Avenue de Wagram are used for guests, advanced students, and carefully staged privacy. The large salon is where recitals, readings, and small exhibitions are held. Bedrooms nearby are comfortable but plainly watched: foot traffic in the gallery makes true solitude difficult.

The service side of the house is more complicated than guests realize. Offices, kitchens, linen rooms, a service stair, small courtyards, and narrow passages let staff move invisibly through the building. Students quickly learn that the house has two versions: the one seen by patrons, and the one used by people carrying keys.

## Staff

The house keeps a small but capable staff, large enough to maintain the retreat without making it feel like a hotel. They know when to appear with tea, coal, towels, or fresh paper, and they know when a student should not be left alone with a locked door, a mirror, or a window after dusk.

- **Mr. Silas Burke, Head Butler:** Burke runs the household with a quiet exactness that makes argument feel childish. He receives visitors, controls the keys, manages the staff, and always seems to know which room a guest has entered before anyone tells him. He is courteous to the students but rarely warm.
- **Elise Marceau, Maid:** Elise tends the bedrooms, gallery dusting, linens, and the small domestic messes caused by nervous young artists. She is young enough that students underestimate her and experienced enough to notice what they hide under mattresses, behind canvases, and inside folded letters.
- **Madame Voclain, Cook:** Madame Voclain rules the kitchen and service passages with practical authority. She feeds the children well, complains about waste, and keeps a private ledger of who eats, who refuses food, and who asks for strange things at strange hours.
- **Henri Belloc, Porter:** Henri watches the door, receives parcels, and handles trunks from arriving families. He has the broad stillness of a man who has removed more than one guest from a drawing room without raising his voice.
- **Clara Dumesnil, Night Attendant:** Clara sits awake after the lamps are lowered, ostensibly to answer bells and tend fires. In truth, she listens for sleepwalking, weeping, and the sound of footsteps where no one should be walking.

:::gm
The father's suicide should remain ambiguous until needed: possible causes include debt, artistic failure, a forbidden painting, or something he saw in his son's earliest work. The mother's comfort may not have been healing so much as fixation. The North Star symbol can recur as a mark on paintings, invitations, student work, and doors that should not be open.

The floorplan makes the house useful for pursuit and secrecy. The main courtyard is visible from too many rooms, the galleries create long sight lines, and the service stair lets staff appear ahead of intruders. The shuttered stair may connect to the room where the father died, an attic studio, or a concealed passage to a space not shown on the plan.

At least one member of the staff should be genuinely protective of the children, and at least one should report everything to the owner without question. Burke is the obvious watcher, but Elise may be the better source of truth if the characters earn her trust.
