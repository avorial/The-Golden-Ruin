# House of the North Star

![The discreet street frontage of the House of the North Star.](/wiki/media/HotN-Street-View.png)

## Location

The House of the North Star stands at the corner of Rue Théodule-Ribot and Avenue de Wagram, on the Plaine-de-Monceaux edge of Paris's [[17th arrondissement]]. It is close enough to the Étoile and Place des Ternes for wealthy patrons to arrive without inconvenience, but removed from the grand hotels and public theatres where every arrival might be noticed.

The retreat occupies the principal floors of an imposing corner residence. Its elevation above the street muffles much of the noise from Wagram, though the sound of horses and carriage wheels still rises from the avenue at busy hours.

The neighborhood suits the house perfectly: expensive, orderly, and watchful. Parc Monceau lies within comfortable walking distance, while cabs, omnibuses, and the great radial avenues provide easy access to the Arc de Triomphe, the Champs-Élysées, private clubs, salons, galleries, and the circles frequented by wealthy foreign visitors and American expatriates.

The house therefore feels central without being exposed. A student may be taken to a gallery opening, a patron's dinner, or a concert and returned before midnight without ever passing through the noisier quarters of Paris.

The same geography makes escape deceptively easy. Wagram and Ternes promise crowds, cabs, and anonymity only a few streets away. Reaching them without being noticed by the household is another matter.


## Overview

The House of the North Star is a private artists' retreat hidden behind the respectable façade of a wealthy Parisian residence.

From the street there is little to distinguish it from its neighbors: carved limestone, wrought-iron balconies, shuttered windows, polished doors, and the correct silence expected of an expensive household. Only a small eight-pointed star worked into the iron above the entrance suggests that the building has a name at all.

Inside, the house opens around courtyards, galleries, stairways, and long corridors designed as much for separation and observation as for comfort.

Among certain old families, the North Star is known as a place where unusual young talent may be disciplined, refined, and protected from vulgar attention. Its master, **Lucien de Vauclair**, inherited the property following the suicide of his father, **Armand de Vauclair**, and gradually transformed what had been a house of mourning into a retreat for painters, musicians, poets, and children whose gifts had become inconvenient to their families.

Among its patrons, discretion is the house's greatest virtue.

A family may send a son who hears music no tutor can explain, a daughter whose sketches disturb dinner guests, or an heir whose eccentricities have begun to attract the wrong sort of attention. The North Star promises education, quiet, culture, and the company of others born beneath the same cold privileges.

No sign advertises the retreat. No prospectus is printed.

One is invited.


## History

The modern history of the house begins with **Armand de Vauclair**, a wealthy financier and collector whose fortune grew alongside the new neighborhoods west of central Paris.

When the corner property became available, Armand acquired it and commissioned an imposing residence suited to a family that had risen quickly enough to require architecture capable of suggesting greater antiquity.

He made one unusual decision.

Rather than give the residence the Vauclair name, he retained an older name associated with the land:

**L'Étoile du Nord.**

For several years the Vauclairs lived there comfortably. Armand entertained businessmen, collectors, minor aristocrats, and artists. His wife, **Cécile de Vauclair, née Delorme**, was remembered as gracious and unusually private. Their only son, **Lucien**, was quiet, observant, and already showing considerable talent as a painter.

Then Armand killed himself.

No satisfactory explanation was ever made public.

Lucien was still young when his father died, leaving behind shuttered rooms, unanswered correspondence, unfinished financial affairs, and the peculiar silence that settles over a household in which the servants know more than the guests.

Cécile was devastated.

Her greatest comfort came from the paintings her son began producing for her. At first they were small northern landscapes: frozen harbors, bare trees, distant villages, dark water, and windows glowing against snow.

Gradually the skies became more important than the landscapes beneath them.

Again and again, Lucien painted the same solitary star.

Cécile would sit before these pictures for hours. She claimed they gave her something fixed upon which to concentrate when memory became unbearable.

Guests visiting during those years remembered Lucien as courteous, pale, and unnervingly patient. He behaved less like a grieving child than the master of a convalescent household, always ready with tea, a blanket, another candle, or another painting for his mother.

The star became his private mark.

Years later, Lucien traveled extensively, spending considerable time among wealthy families in Britain and America. There he discovered something that would eventually give the house its present purpose.

Great families possessed children they did not understand.

Some were artistic.

Some were merely difficult.

Others dreamed things, drew things, heard things, or said things that no respectable drawing room could comfortably contain.

Lucien began offering a solution with the mild confidence of a man proposing a charitable arrangement.

Let the child come to Paris for a season.

Let them study.

Let them be surrounded by beauty, discipline, and others like themselves.

Let them learn to make something useful from whatever strange faculty Providence had given them.

The first invitations were personal favors, offered after dinners, exhibitions, concerts, and long conversations with parents who had grown tired of apologizing for their children.

By the second year, families were requesting places before Lucien offered them.

The House of the North Star ceased to be merely a private residence.

It became an understanding.


## The Name

The name **North Star** is considerably older than the Vauclair family.

Long before Rue Théodule-Ribot existed, when this part of Paris was still fields, roads, gardens, and scattered buildings beyond the old city, an inn supposedly stood somewhere near the present corner.

Its name was **L'Auberge de l'Étoile du Nord**, the Inn of the North Star.

Local tradition claimed that its proprietor had once been connected to the Knights Templar. Later versions of the story named him **Matthieu d'Aubrac**, a former Templar sergeant, astrologer, and reputed mystic who settled outside Paris after the suppression of the Order.

Whether such a man actually existed is difficult to establish.

The stories about him are easier to find.

D'Aubrac was said to chart the movement of stars from an upper room of the inn. Travelers occasionally claimed that he could tell them where they were going before they told him where they had come from. Others said certain guests arrived at the inn after becoming lost on roads they had traveled their entire lives.

The inn burned one winter night.

Accounts disagree about the year.

They also disagree about whether anyone escaped.

One persistent version claims that neighbors arrived while the building was still burning and found its front door standing open, though the doorway itself was untouched by flame. Another claims that every window had been shuttered from inside.

The building disappeared.

The name did not.

For centuries afterward, fragments of the story remained attached to the land. A tavern, garden, stable, or house built nearby would occasionally acquire some variation of *Étoile du Nord* as a nickname. Fires, disappearances, strange lights, and unfortunate deaths were periodically added to the legend, usually decades after they supposedly occurred.

Most educated Parisians regarded the entire history as neighborhood folklore.

Armand de Vauclair did not.

When he acquired the property, he deliberately revived the old name.

He had a small North Star incorporated into the ironwork of the new residence and referred to the property privately as **L'Hôtel de l'Étoile du Nord**.

After Armand's suicide, Cécile found a different meaning in the name.

She told Lucien that his paintings had become her north star: not warmth, not happiness, but direction.

Lucien never changed the name.

When he eventually opened the house to young artists, it became almost too appropriate.

To patrons, the North Star suggests guidance, steadiness, education, and noble purpose.

To the students, it carries a different promise:

One may be lost without being abandoned.

The house cannot remove the darkness.

It can teach them how to navigate by it.


## House & Grounds

![Ground floor plan of the House of the North Star.](/wiki/media/HotN-Floorplan.png)

The North Star is much larger than its street entrance suggests.

The residence is arranged around a principal courtyard, with reception rooms, bedrooms, galleries, service passages, and smaller courts surrounding the central open space. The architecture creates the peculiar impression that every room is connected to every other room, though reaching the room one wants may require crossing half the house.

The main courtyard is the visual center of the residence.

Sound behaves strangely there.

A single piano note played in the salon may be heard by someone crossing near the kitchens. A conversation on the landing sometimes seems to come from an upper floor. A whisper spoken beside an open window may travel farther than a shout.

Students eventually learn not to discuss secrets beside the courtyard.

The western side of the house contains the formal rooms: the dining room, grand salon, petit salon, and the chambers used when patrons are received. These are the rooms visitors see first, and they present precisely the image Lucien wishes the house to project.

Tasteful.

Cultivated.

Controlled.

The long gallery forms the principal spine of the residence, connecting the public rooms to the private bedrooms, salons, courtyards, and service areas. Movement through the house is therefore surprisingly easy to observe.

The rooms overlooking Avenue de Wagram are among the finest in the residence and are generally reserved for guests, advanced students, or those whose families expect a certain standard of accommodation.

The great salon is used for recitals, readings, lectures, and private exhibitions. On important evenings, chairs are arranged in rows and paintings displayed along the walls while servants circulate quietly with wine and refreshments.

The bedrooms nearby are comfortable but offer less privacy than they initially appear to. Nearly everyone entering or leaving must eventually cross the gallery.

The service side of the residence is more complicated than most guests realize.

Kitchens, offices, linen rooms, servants' stairs, small courtyards, and narrow passages allow the staff to move through the house without crossing its formal rooms.

Students quickly discover that the North Star contains two different buildings.

There is the house seen by patrons.

And there is the house used by people carrying keys.


## Staff

The North Star maintains a small but capable household staff, large enough to keep the retreat functioning without allowing it to resemble a hotel.

They know when to appear with tea, coal, towels, fresh paper, or another lamp.

They also know when a student should not be left alone with a locked door, a mirror, or an open window after dusk.

- **[[Silas Burke, Head Butler]]:** Burke governs the household with such quiet exactness that argument with him quickly feels childish. He receives visitors, controls the keys, assigns servants, and always seems to know which room a guest has entered before anyone tells him. He is unfailingly courteous to the students but rarely affectionate.

- **Élise Marceau, Maid:** Élise tends the bedrooms, gallery, linens, and the thousand minor domestic disasters produced by nervous young artists. She is young enough that students underestimate her and experienced enough to know exactly what they hide beneath mattresses, behind canvases, and inside folded letters.

- **Madame Eugénie Voclain, Cook:** Madame Voclain rules the kitchens and adjoining service passages with practical authority. She feeds the students well, despises waste, and maintains a private ledger recording who eats, who refuses food, who sleeps through breakfast, and who asks for peculiar things at peculiar hours.

- **Henri Belloc, Porter:** Henri controls the outer door, receives parcels, handles luggage, summons cabs, and deals with tradesmen. He possesses the broad stillness of a man who has removed more than one unwelcome visitor from a drawing room without raising his voice.

- **Clara Dumesnil, Night Attendant:** Clara remains awake after the lamps have been lowered, ostensibly to answer bells, tend fires, and assist any student who becomes ill. Her more important duty is listening: for sleepwalking, crying, doors opening, and footsteps in parts of the house where no one should be walking.

### The Vauclair Family

**Lucien de Vauclair** is the present master of the North Star.

His father, **Armand de Vauclair**, purchased the property, revived the North Star name, and later killed himself within the house.

His mother, **Cécile de Vauclair, née Delorme**, survived her husband but became increasingly dependent upon Lucien and his paintings. Whether the paintings genuinely comforted her or merely replaced one obsession with another should remain uncertain.

Armand's suicide should not initially have a definitive explanation. Possibilities include financial trouble, an affair, artistic obsession, something discovered during the construction of the house, or something seen in Lucien's earliest paintings.

The older history of the North Star should be equally uncertain.

There may genuinely have been a Templar named Matthieu d'Aubrac.

There may have been an inn.

There was almost certainly a fire.

Everything beyond that becomes progressively harder to prove.

Old newspaper clippings, parish records, family correspondence, and neighborhood stories should contradict one another in small but troubling ways.

The North Star symbol should recur throughout the campaign: on Lucien's paintings, invitations, stationery, student sketches, ironwork, old masonry, and occasionally on doors where nobody remembers seeing it before.

### The House

The floor plan makes the North Star particularly useful for pursuit, surveillance, and secrecy.

The main courtyard can be observed from numerous rooms. The galleries provide long sight lines. The service stairs allow servants to move faster than guests who do not understand the building.

Someone fleeing through the public rooms may discover that Burke is already waiting for them at the next door.

There should also be parts of the North Star that do not correspond cleanly with the architectural plan.

A shuttered stair might lead to the room where Armand died.

A locked upper studio might contain Lucien's earliest paintings.

A servants' passage might continue several yards farther than the exterior dimensions of the building appear to permit.

At least one member of the staff should sincerely care for the students.

At least one should report everything to Lucien without hesitation.

Burke is the obvious watcher.

Élise may prove far more useful to anyone who actually wants the truth.
